When I was younger, I thought the purpose of warming up was to prevent injury. Well when I was much younger, I thought warm-ups were pointless wastes of time that grown-ups made kids do before playing soccer. One coach even admitted it. He said we’d only really need them when we were old and stiff, which is what I remembered when I was sprinting during high school track practice in February and heard my hamstring pop.

It was a memorable enough lesson that I never reconsidered the purpose of warming up despite lots of pickleball and improv, in which injury, though not unheard of, is rare, and the prevailing warm-ups do nothing to prevent it[^pickleball]. Honestly, I may have even reverted back to warming up when and how everyone else did. It wasn’t until I joined a choir and started playing tennis that I gave any more thought to it.

[^pickleball]: Admittedly pickleball injuries aren’t that rare, but that has more to do with the sport's demographics. Still, the only way many players warm up is with a little dinking which is especially maddening when dinking isn’t a very big part of their game.

I was amazed at how well the choir warm-up prepared me to sing. I could hit notes and intervals more accurately. I was ready to switch into head voice or sustain a long note. I wasn’t worrying about work or my own problems. I was focused on the music and excited to sing[^repertoire].

[^repertoire]: In this case, the Cherubini Requiem in C Minor

Tennis was the opposite. I had no coach[^heather] and no routine. For a while I thought I was simply an inconsistent player (much the same way I’m still an inconsistent singer in the car). Some days my shots would go in all kinds of surprising directions. It took me a long time to realize I wasn't watching the ball, turning my hips, stepping into a shot, or following through. I didn’t get injured because I wasn’t even moving to the ball enough, much less pushing any kind of limits. It’s embarrassing how much a little jog beforehand improved my game.

[^heather]: One of the go-to small talk topics among choir members was how great the director was.

Warm-ups have at least four distinct effects. There's the physical muscle recruitment that gets the cold and stiffness out. Your muscles literally get warm[^misdirect]. There's a separate neurological effect that calibrates the relevant complex movements ([Ajemian et al. 2010](https://web.mit.edu/ajemian/www/Ajemian_et_al_Motor_learning.pdf)). There's an attentional effect that redirects focus from whatever you were thinking about onto the relevant features of the task at hand. Finally there's a motivational effect. Warm-ups excite you to engage in the activity.

[^misdirect]: I don't know the biology behind this. I'll misdirect by saying that the difference between warm- and cold-blooded animals is that warm-blooded animals are less efficient at cellular respiration and lose more energy to heat. But hey, I ain't complainin'!

Whereas the physical effect gets the large muscles to generate power, the neurological effect is what gets all the various muscle groups to work together in concert. In the case of tennis, this would be micro-adjustments given the surface and conditions to be able to hit the spot your aiming for accurately. This is also distinct from the attentional effect which is at the level of conscious thought. You're actively watching the ball.

Motivation has more to do with how you feel about an activity than how the warm-up goes, but it's still more flexible than people imagine. I wouldn't need any warm-up to get excited about a bowl of ice cream, but most days I need a mile or 2 to get excited about running. The benefit of a warm-up is that it starts out easy. It's easy to stretch, and once you've stretched it's easy to jog, and pretty soon those hill repeats don't actually sound too bad. I never wake up wanting to clean the bathroom, but if I wash the dishes, I might notice the kitchen floor needs a sweep, and suddenly the bathroom is clean too.

Now many of those effects would happen whether or not you warmed up. We ought to be looking at the difference between warming up and not. The main effect of skipping a warm-up is not that you don't get warm. It's that you warm up slower or inconsistently. In a game of tennis, you might not hit many backhands (especially if you're missing your shots). It could take you a whole set just to warm up your muscles and calibrate the shot. It would be much more even and consistent if you hit 20 in a row right at the start. And that's not even considering the possibility of an uneven warm-up.

With an uneven warm-up, some of these effects might take less time than others. The highest risk of injury is when the muscular preparation lags the motivational. It wasn’t much of a concern for me hitting tennis balls around because I wasn’t even inclined to move my feet to a shot, much less sprint after a ball. As a competitive, energetic high-schooler, though, I was ready to give 100% to practice even in the cold. If you calibrate your shots with cold muscles, the aim will change as your muscles warm up. Even within a single effect, the warm-up could be uneven. Some muscle groups could be warmer than others or you might remember to watch the ball but not to follow-through.

Of course warm-ups aren’t magical. At best they bring you to the edge of your current ability. You can't recruit muscles beyond what you've strengthened. A great warm-up might help me get balls in the court, but it wouldn't help me hit a target smaller than a dinner table. I'd need to practice more.

True to form, as soon as I noticed the benefit to my tennis game, I became interested in whether a warm-up could improve my writing. I want to believe Jerry Seinfeld that Writer’s Block doesn't exist, but it's such a convenient way to fancy myself a creative without any actual work (and only occasional self-loathing). From there I began to wonder if there was anything else in my life that a warm-up could benefit. Is there a good warm-up for reading, juggling, tutoring, or a job interview? Is that even a sensical question?

A warm-up is a procedure that prepares someone for an activity. It’s usually a routine that consists of multiple exercises that are tailored to the specific activity. Tennis warm-ups prepare you for tennis and not for soccer. A warm-up usually happens directly before the activity in question. It is a preparation of the self rather than any equipment. One test to distinguish warm-ups from other types of preparations is whether someone else can do them for you. Someone can drive you to the courts and tie your shoes. They could even warm up your squash ball for you, but you have to warm yourself up.

Not everything you do right before an activity is a warm-up though. Procrastination is commonly mistaken for warming up. Dawdling by the side of the pool does not prepare you to swim laps except in that you can only dawdle for so long.

Warm-ups are almost exclusively used for sports and performance. The obvious common thread is that both have stakes. It only matters what happens when the curtain comes up, so you do what you can beforehand to make sure it goes well right from the start. The stakes don't have to be so high either. You warm up your car in the Winter because it matters how hot it is from the moment you start driving. The time idling in the driveway doesn't "count". This is the origin of the traditional joke that the first game is a warm-up, implying it shouldn't count against the losers.

There are even stakes to practice. They're just about having an effective practice rather than winning or performing well. There may also be some argument that warming up for practice is also a practice for warming up. If you warm up to reliably achieve a certain state, then it makes sense to practice achieving that state and adjust as needed.

I don’t want to wander too far from one set of half-baked ideas to another, so I’ll quickly propose three mental models for good practices. First, good practice usually occurs around the edge of current ability. Drills that are too easy don’t extend your ability, and you can’t get a handle on too difficult ones. In practice, the optimal amount of failure is between 0 and 100%. If a warm-up is a way to return to the current edge of your ability, then it does prepare you for that appropriately challenging practice. Under this view, warm-ups form the foundation for the practice session. Getting ready to run and watch the ball prepares you to practice your backhand. Calibrating your backhand prepares you to hit strategic positioning shots.

Another way of looking at practice is in terms of consistency. If peak performance is being able to perform at a high level reliably, then much of practice is about becoming more consistent. As I said, if you skip a warm-up, you’ll still gradually get warm as you play, but it won’t necessarily be an even warm-up. This is when you might pull a muscle because you’re motivated to sprint before your muscles are ready or your muscles might be warm without ever being focused on the ball. Some muscles might even get warm faster than others. Rather than simple distraction, you might actively be focusing on the wrong thing. (I find that the moment my attention shifts from simply watching the ball to [imagining how hard I’m gonna smash it](../narcissism), I majorly whiff). At best you learn slower; at worst you learn bad habits. A routine of exercises that gradually ratchet up both intensity and complexity is likely a faster and more effective way of warming up than simply playing, and a practice session that you start warm will itself be more effective because you’re not adjusting as you go or compensating.

Finally, the challenge of improving at any complex skill is that it's only possible to consciously focus on one, maybe two tiny aspects at a time. The way to learn any juggling pattern is to practice each individual type of throw on its own a bunch of times until it's second nature. Then practice each combination ad nauseam. When it first "clicks" you're able to consciously think about one throw and your hands involuntarily do the other ones (usually still dropping a lot). If you try to think about all the throws at once, you will drop everything. Ideally, the warm-up will allow those other components to happen automatically. Our choir warm-ups included exercises for hitting various notes and intervals, pure vowel sounds, putting final consonants in the right place, breath support, dynamics and head voice, so that each of these things would happen automatically while we were focused on reading the correct notes, rhythms, and Latin while attending to the director and other voices.

The only sport I know without a warm-up is amateur marathoning[^fellrnr]. Using the beginning of the race to warm up helps avoid starting too fast. Standing at a marathon start is adrenaline-inducing enough that having cold muscles is a legitimate strategic hack. Besides, much of long distance success in getting enough water, salt, and calories; pre-race exertion complicates that. To be pedantic though, some stretching is still probably[^brendan] a good idea. It’s less a question of whether to warm up and more of how much.

[^fellrnr]: https://fellrnr.com/wiki/Warmup#Warming_up_for_the_marathon. Presumably there's similar logic for other edurance events.

[^brendan]: Brendan Leonard *30 Questions You May (Or May Not) Have About Ultrarunning* at [14:53](https://youtu.be/dyT8wkbMbLE?t=893)

This mirrors activities that are absurd to imagine warming up for like commuting and cooking (except pancakes famously). You could chop a practice onion but then you might as well just use it and it ceases to be a warm-up. You could drive around the block, but that's more a waste of gas than anything.

The performing arts exception that comes to mind is stand-up comedy. In this case, standups could use a warm-up, and based on a quick search, some are desperate for one, but there’s no consensus (on Reddit). Unsurprisingly, half the people in r/Standup are jokesters. Of the serious answers, many of them say to drink. Some admit to not having a preshow routine. The helpful ones advise experimenting with different things to find something that works for you (gee, thanks). Even the fact that people are searching for this kind of help on Reddit is telling.

Even if you're not afflicted with the disease of compulsive trying to improve at everything you do all the time, it can be nice to try to make everyday activities go well. Inversely, it can be frustrating to do anything worse than you know you're able. There can still be stakes for a friendly game going well even without improvement as a goal. There are also a few non-sports or performance activities that people warm up for by different names. There's winding down for bed, pregaming, and foreplay (though if "performance" is a euphemism for sex, it's barely an exception). People wind down before bed not because they want to get better at sleeping but just to sleep better and faster that night.

However there are also lots of nonphysical activities with stakes that don't seem to have a warm-up. These are things like exams[^exams], chess tournaments, spelling bees, public speaking, and job interviews, pretty much anything that people will tell you to take deep breaths before. Deep breaths are a key indicator that people care about the outcome and intuitively believe there should be some warm-up but don't know what else to try[^cuddy]. In my experience, if you even have the wherewithal to remember the option of deep breaths you're already calm enough to not need them.

[^exams]: I had an elementary school teacher who would always implore us to "read something, anything, even the back of the cereal box" on mornings before standardized tests. Maybe that counts.

[^cuddy]: See also: power posing. If you’re not familiar, one of the most viewed TED Talks is Amy Cuddy's on striking superhero poses before job interviews to make you feel more confident and perform better. When I heard it wasn’t replicated, my gut reaction was something like, “How could I have been so stupid as to believe that? There must be nothing you can do to be more confident in a job interview.” I suspect the problem has more to do with people not doing many job interviews. It's not something you practice and improve at (unless you have a job coach).

It's understandable when you consider that the physical component makes it more obvious that you're not warmed up. Tennis has a very salient and immediate feedback mechanism in that you can see where the ball went compared with where you wanted it to go. It's hard to even pinpoint the analogue for something like chess. I always feel totally focused the moment I sit down in an uncomfortable chair in a big room with hundreds of other players, but I've also made plenty of stupid blunders.

The other difference is that activities with warm-ups are more social than activities without. This has more to do with the way that warm-ups are learned and transmitted via coaches and teams rather than anything about the activities. Online, various people do suggest warm-ups for chess tournaments, but they don't seem to have caught on broadly (hint: they include deep breaths). Like me, most people warm up when and how they're told and don't give it much more thought. This is why our standup comedians don't have a good warm-up. It's the most solitary performance. That said, it should still be possible to construct a good warm-up if we know the goal state.

Mirroring the model for good practice, a good warm-up breaks a complex action down into its smallest component parts. Take high jump for instance. A successful high jump requires the jumper to build up speed on in a straight line, maintain that speed along a tight curve while leaning away from the bar, jump off one foot while twisting every which way, and bend their back at the right moment as their momentum carries them over the bar if they’ve done everything right. To warm up for high jump, the jumper isolates each of these movements as much as possible. They do standing back flops over the bar. They run through the approach. They pop-up or do an upright “scissors kick” over the bar. Each of these cues them to different parts of the complex whole so that they can all happen more or less automatically while they focus on maximizing the force they can drive through their jumping foot into the ground without messing with the delicate balance.

Another way to structure a warm-up is to build in intensity. Not only does the whole routine prepare you for peak performance, but each step prepares you for the next. Improv warm-ups typically begin with exercises that demand no thought, only high energy and physicality. Then there are warm-ups that require energy and listening to what others are saying. Then warm-ups that require those things plus lowering inhibitions, saying the first thing that comes to mind. Then there might be bespoke exercises for the particular type of improv you’re doing.

Things aren't looking too good for writing at this point. There are no stakes. You can always backspace. There's not much physical complexity or intensity. If it were a sport it would be jogging (call me biased). The only hope is that there's a way to attain a state of attention that's good for writing.

Another problem is that I've underspecified what I actually want. I want to write more, I want to enjoy writing more, and I want the writing itself to be better. I'd implicitly assumed that these would form a virtuous cycle, but getting really good at almost anything requires putting in some work that isn't enjoyable. This brings me to the limitations of warm-ups.

Warming up is only useful when you've already committed to do something and set aside time for it. I can sing whenever the impulse strikes me, but when I want to sing with other people, for boring logistical reasons, we can't just do it whenever I feel like it. Therefore, the true power of the choir warm-up is not just in getting me ready to sing but in getting 100 people ready to sing every Wednesday night. Despite knowing these great warm-ups, I don't sing as much anymore because I'm not in the choir. If I wanted to sing more, I wouldn't be racking my brain about warm-ups. I would just join another choir.

It seems like forcing myself to write more would conflict with enjoying it more. Feeling excited to write is so entangled with the feeling of having something to write about. Thankfully that's not a problem for me who has epiphanies daily. Besides, it's similar to running. Some runs are horrible; some are good; some are sublime, and if I only ran when I felt like it, I would miss out on a lot of great runs. But I still value the bad runs because they confer the same cardiovascular benefits, and they make it easier to get out the door the next time.

One of the notorious perennial blogger topics is that "writing is thinking". The argument usually focuses on the revision process during which clarifying your words clarifies your thoughts. I don't disagree (except in the case of this post which I've spent a lot of time writing without my thoughts becoming clear), but I also think it underrates the value of the first draft. The first draft forces me to actually attempt a coherent train of thought rather than turning the same sentence over and over in my head. The similarity with running holds. Unless you're moving forward, you're not actually doing it.

Warm-ups shift attention by reframing success. Whereas normally in tennis, you succeed by hitting the ball somewhere on the court where your opponent can't return it, during a warm-up, you succeed by moving your feet correctly and hitting a specific shot with good form. A favorite improv warm-up, 30 Things, redefines success from the everyday definition: "don't make a fool of yourself" to "name 30 things in a category as fast as you can". I'll stop just shy of reinventing Julia Cameron's Morning Pages which also redefine success as writing 3 pages as fast as possible and leave you with the shadowy memory of a book character I can't quite remember who was described as being unable to write a shopping list without imagining how posterity would perceive him (please tell me if this is someone you recognize).

It's not hard to shift your attention towards something you're interested in writing about. Just go for a walk. The challenge of writing is to remain focused on what happens next and what you want to communicate without getting distracted by the coffeeshop women talking about Montessori schools (like today, unsuccessful) or how annoyingly far the words are from the perfect form in your head (every day, mixed results). That's the other limitation of warm-ups. They can get you started, but they can't keep you going which is most of what writing seems to be.

If that's an unsatisfying answer, here are some bonus warm-ups I've come up with for other activities. Light stretching is recommended before them all. First, to warm up for a job interview, assuming you actually are somewhat prepared and somewhat qualified, go ask a friend or stranger the questions you expect to be asked and see how much more prepared and qualified you are. This comes with the added benefit of putting you in the position of power. While you're at it, try to describe to them exactly what you think the job would be like. Anything you realize you don't understand is something you could ask the interviewer about.

The warm-up for karaoke is much different than for choir. Try to sing some other people's songs under your breath to prepare yourself for that surprise bridge (or, god forbid, verses) that you don't know the tune of.

Since a healthy back is important for hiking, a good warm-up is to pick a prominent tree at the trailhead, scan it from bottom to top. It should be tall enough so that, when your eyes reach the top, you're considerably bent back. Calibrate your sightlines by finding the farthest point you can see in the distance (could be quite close or far depending on your biome). Now you're ready to hike.

The best way to warm up for a jazz concert is to run the last stretch there, so you're (a) a little sweaty and (b) not late but feeling like you might be late. You can close your eyes while you catch your breath and feel your heart beating your body. With any luck, you'll be so glad you made it in time that you'll forget you don't really "understand" jazz. Sorry this is if you're in the audience. If you're playing obviously don't listen to me.

There's no warm-up for reading a book because reading is itself simply a warm-up for falling asleep.

Different people warm up for dancing at weddings in different ways. For some, sitting through a long ceremony all about two people is reminder enough that they're backup dancers and no one's paying attention to them. To others, enjoying dancing is a vaguely horrifying and alien notion they can neither join nor look away from. For them, the key is to blend in with the aliens before they're noticed and dragged onto the floor unwillingly. It doesn't matter if you like it. Just pick someone who looks like they know what they're doing and kind of try to imitate them. Actually maybe that would work for writing too.
